Introduction Origins Iteration Modern Age Futures
The technology of writing has dramatically transformed over the last few thousand years as demand for more accurate documentation and accounting grew, alongside increases in organized governmental power. Writing has the power to illustrate the past, change minds, and help effectively distribute resources. The form and frequency of writing depend significantly on the resources they are made with. This affects how quickly writing can be learned, created, and how easily it can be stored and referenced. This point is especially relevant as we look into how technology has changed the way we use writing throughout its history.

Visualized Information

While there are only a few recorded instances of complete writing systems developing independently (which we will get to shortly), there are a lot more examples of visual systems that represent information in a way that does not directly reflect spoken language. These systems should not be viewed as less developed or less useful. As we’ll see in this example, they have unique advantages that make them attractive for specific purposes. It’s also likely that the writing systems we’re about to look at including, Cuneiform, Hieroglyphics, and the Chinese Script, developed from one of these more abstract systems. So, while these systems are interesting in their own right, they can give us an idea of the roles early writing played in different societies and how the materials used to make them affect how they are used and preserved.


Incan Quipus
1400 - 1600 CE, Peru
Quipus (Khipus), meaning Knot in Quechua, have existed since at least the advent of the Incan Empire in 1400 CE and possibly beforehand. Quipus are our only primary source for pre-Columbian Incan empire. The exact meaning of each preserved quipu is not well understood since translations between quipus and known written languages have not been found. It’s believed that Quipus documented numerical information but also spoken words to some extend (This was not common of all knot mnemonics). Quipus are still used today, though they have been modified with time. This use might be partially due to the benefit ropes have over written language as a more durable material.
Advantages of Ropes
Quipus are made with various types of rope material, I think mostly cotton, alpaca fur, and sometimes human hair, which could be used as a sort of signature identifying the creator. Because knots can easily be tied and retied, Quipus can be created and edited easily. Ropes are also fairly easy and cheap to create. They are durable and light making them easy to transport. These qualities made them attractive in harsh environments like the Andes mountains.
Uses of Quipus
Like the majority of writing systems throughout history, Quipus were likely used for resource management purposes, documenting stuff like crop yields and trades of livestock or artisan goods. Because Quipus were sometimes made with human hair, scientists have been able to test the strands to get an idea of the diet the writers may have regularly eaten. The results indicated that Quipus writers typically had a high-carb and fiber diet, suggesting they may not have been upper-class, as previously assumed. Because of this, it's suggested that literacy in the Incan Empire may have been more widespread than it was in many other places at this time.

Origins of Phonetic Writing

All known modern scripts developed from large systems of detailed characters that communicated meanings without directly representing speech. It took a long time for early writing systems to truly reflect the spoken word. Most early written artifacts are more index than literary text. They served a very specific purpose: to record resources often for trade, planning, and distribution. Because writing systems needed to represent many concepts without representing their sound, scripts were comprised of hundreds of characters that each represent a specific concept. Partially because of this, these systems took years to learn, and literacy rates were very low amongst the general public. Writing was almost exclusively used by the ruling class and could easily fall out of use with a change in power.

Mesopotamian Cuneiform
3000-1000 BCE, Western Asia
Cuneiform is famous as the first alphabet in known history, giving us a window into the lives and stories of people over 5000 years ago. Cuneiform writing systems first appeared along the tigris and euphrates rivers, in modern day Iraq. As the script developed into a tool for accurate documentation of spoken language, it was adopted by many different civilizations from as far north as modern-day Turkey (with the Hittite script) and as far east as modern-day Iran (with the Old Persian script). Cuneiform iterations were eventually abandoned, possibly due to their inefficiency, since they typically took longer to write compared to hieroglyphics. Though we should be careful not to assume its abandonment was purely failure of Cuneiform to conform to subsequent demands. Language and writing changes are more frequently a response to political change. Changes can also be pretty arbitrary.
Materials
The script, cuneiform, was most often written on clay tablets. Clay was cheap and easy to collect and dry, making it an attractive material for writing on. It also preserves better than other materials used in the ancient world, like wood or papyrus, which likely biases our understanding of materials used to some extent.
Early Uses
Early records of Cuneiform tablets were pictographic ledgers of sketched and labeled items for trade. “These early clay tablets show at least fifteen hundred different pictograms and symbols, each standing for one concrete object.” (Fischer 29) These records were created by the Sumerians around the city of Uruk (modern-day south-west Iraq).
Writing Utensil
The reed stylus used to write cuneiform was cut out into a wedge shape, creating the recognizable triangle-shaped strokes that made up cuneiform script.
Laws in Writing
While Cuneiform was originally used for the Sumerian language, it spread to other communities like the Babylonians, who wrote the famous Code of Hammurabi in the late 18th century BCE, carving the rules into stone obelisks. Codes described crimes and their given punishments, including: “ If any one steal the property of a temple or of the court, he shall be put to death, and also the one who receives the stolen thing from him shall be put to death.” Unfortunately, most offenders were illiterate.

Indus Valley Script


A Script For An Unknown Language
The Indus Script is probably the most infamous undeciphered script, written during the equally mysterious Indus Empire located in Modern Pakistan. It’s striking how, even though the script was in use for nearly a thousand years, the script eventually fell out of use and memory. Looking closer at the history of global writing systems, it's common for alphabets to fall out of use; sometimes this follows a large change in societal structure, sometimes the cause is less obvious from a modern perspective. Maintaining a writing system requires time (learning to read and write), resources, and demand, which have not always been deemed a worthy cost.
Known Artifacts
The majority of artifacts displaying the Indus script appear on clay and copper seals alongside illustrated livestock. This suggests the writings were used to facilitate trade, as was the case in many nearby civilizations. While the origins of the script are unknown, experts suggest the script may resemble the proto-Elamite script, an early child system of cuneiform. The types of artifacts discovered are likely not accurate representations of all the materials used to write the script (this will become a trend in the following examples). Scholars believe the script may have also appeared on papyrus and/or wood, but those artifacts have not been preserved as well over thousands of years.
Oracle Bones
1600-1100 BCE, China
While China has one of the longest and most significant writing traditions in history, its origins are somewhat unknown. Some scholars previously suggest that it originally developed from Cuneiform due to some similarities in characters. However, the majority seem to agree that these influences were minimal since Chinese characters didn’t share the semi-phonetic traits of Cuneiform scripts from the same period. Bone artifacts displaying the script are dated from around 1600 BCE to 1100 BCE during the Shang Dynasty. The script found on these artifacts is not believed to be a particularly early development of the script due to the linguistic complexity observable.
Uses
The bone artifacts displaying this script are believed to be used for predictive purposes where they were used by the upper class. The earliest preserved version of Chinese writing appears on large, flat bones, mostly ox scapulae and turtle carapaces. The bones would be placed under heat, where they would crack. The details of said cracks could be read to make predictions. Oracle bones typically included: an introduction giving context to the moment of prediction, the topic that would be predicted upon, an analysis of the cracks, and a report of the actual event that was predicted as it occurred. This format made oracle bones a very valuable source for historical information.
Materials
Bone inscriptions were first written with ink. Marks were then carved into bones when the ink was placed. The use of ink suggests the script may have also been used on paper and/or bamboo at the time and for a long time before these documents appeared, but similar to the Indus script, these materials did not preserve. Because characters had to be carved in bone, line strokes maintained a fairly uniform width and were generally less delicate compared to later calligraphy styles of writing Chinese characters. Later artifacts began to display a more organic appearance due to the softer material used to make them, for instance, wax cast for metal seals and ink on wood.

Hieroglyphics


3000-1000 BCE, Egypt
The Egyptian Hieroglyphs are ancestors of the majority of today’s phonetic scripts, ranging from the Latin script to Arabic, Devanagari, and Hangul, among many others. The first phonetic iterations of the script are dated to around 3100 BCE. It’s debated whether Hieroglyphics were the direct descendant of Cuneiform, even though they came from nearby civilizations around the same time, because they share minimal similarities in appearance and representation of sounds. Cuneiform scripts were syllabaries, representing entire syllables per characters while hieroglyphic scripts were initially consonantal, representing only consonants with characters. This difference was likely the result of differences in the languages for which they developed. However, the relative geographic and temporal closeness of the scripts' development suggests that the creation of Hieroglyphics were at least inspired by the advent of Cuneiform, at around the same time. Like all other early scripts, Hieroglyphics began as a large set of symbols that did not directly reflect the grammar or sounds of the language they represented.
Decorative Script
Egyptian Hieroglyphics were sometimes used for decorative purposes, appearing on temples and other high-status buildings of the period. This was the type of writing we typically picture today, as hieroglyphics, though it was not the form the writing system took during daily use. This decorative form of the language could be viewed as the first example of a calligraphic tradition that's now prominent in many societies (ie, Chinese and Arabic calligraphy).

Iterations of Writing Systems

As phonetic scripts started to develop near the Mediterranean, it quickly spread to nearby civilizations.
None of the thousands of scripts in use today are wholly original. Most decent for a long line of writing systems going back thousands of years. The Phonetian alphabet is often credited as the first completely phonetic alphabet (though it only represented consonants as letterform, making it an abjad). But what really makes the Phoenician alphabet notable from a modern perspective is its role as the predecessor to the vast majority of modern alphabets.

Greece Scripts


1800-1000 BCE, 900 BCE-Present
The influence of Egyptian writing entered Greece and Southern Europe multiple times over thousands of years. The first major Greek script, a syllabary called Linear A, included around 140 characters and appeared mostly on stone and clay seals. Artifacts featuring this script date as far back as 2000 BCE. The last artifacts featuring a Linear script date to around 1200-1100 BCE. For a few hundred years following this, it appears that no writing system was in use, before the beginnings of the modern Greek alphabet entered the region sometime between 1000 BCE and 850 BCE; the exact dates are debated. With time, the letter inventory of Hieroglyphics shrunk and letter forms were simplified, especially the more informal versions of Hieroglyphic, called the Demotic script (from Greek demotikós, or “popular”). The Phoenician alphabet inspired the development of the Greek alphabet, which in turn developed into all popular modern European scripts (Greek, Latin, Cyrilic)
Linear A through C
The earliest versions of Greek writing existed on disks using a system now referred to as Linear A. This system was a syllabary with some remaining non-phonetic characters (logograms), so it had around 120 characters, significantly less than earlier hieroglyphics but more than the modern Greek alphabet.
Dialect Variety
At the time of its invention, there was some conflict over which version of the Greek alphabet should be used. Part of this conflict was due to differences between dialects of the Greek language that required different characters to represent their different sounds.
One of these dialectical varieties became the modern Greek alphabet, another eventually became the Latin script, the most popular script in use today.
Modern Greek
Even as a script resembling the modern Greek alphabet emerged nearly three thousand years ago, many modern writing conventions were not present. For example, throughout most of its history, the alphabet had no lowercase letters, no punctuation, and no spaces between words. Writing direction was also not standardized for some time, so text might be written from right to left or left to right. Most scripts at the time did not have a standard writing direction.
Printing eventually played a part in the standardization of the Greek alphabet and its typographic conventions.
India
200 BCE - Present
Today, South Asia has perhaps the largest variety of modern scripts, all descending from the Brahmi script used in the Ashokan period. It's theorized that Brahmi came from the Aramaic script, which, like Greek, descended from Phoenician.
The majority of Brahmi’s descendants are abugidas, a type of script similar to syllabaries (where each letter represents an entire syllable) except that letterforms beginning with the same consonant share a similar shape that changes slightly to indicate their following vowel.
The great diversity in scripts seen across Southern Asia represents both greater attention directed towards the phonetic representations of each language and a strong association between a script and regional identity.
India’s writing systems have historically held less cultural importance compared to neighboring systems (i.e., Arabic or Chinese scripts) so calligraphic traditions are less robust, with some exceptions (i.e., Siddham letters, used to write a lot of Buddhist manuscripts). The focus on oral documentation instead may have contributed to the diverging appearance of local scripts because of the greater importance placed on accurately representing sounds.
Pillars of Ashoka
These Pillars were built across much of modern India in the Third century BCE to promote Buddhism. Religion was a popular reason for the spread of writing, as we’ll see in some of the following examples. It's unlikely these Pillars were all that effective at spreading Buddhism, since most people were illiterate, but they appear to be more effective at spreading the written tradition.
Tegulu and Kannada
The Brahmi script was also used to represent Dravidian languages, which are not related to Indo-European scripts in the north. They are usually longer and more vowel-heavy. The Tegulu and Kannada scripts developed as a single script, probably around the sixth to eighth century CE, diverging into separate scripts before standardizing with the introduction of the printing press in the nineteenth century.
Tamil
Tamil, another script written in Southern India, has a more boxy appearance, which has been attributed to the palm leaf material the script was traditionally written on. Most letterforms in Tamil contain strokes that start angular before curving because purely angular stroke more prone to ripping the leaves. Tamil also resembles European alphabets more than its neighboring script since it has changed to represent consonant clusters with multiple characters as opposed to the conjunct characters (unique characters representing multiple consonants) used in most Abugidas.
Southeast Asia
The descendants of Brahmi also spread to Southeast Asian countries, some of which are still in use today (ie, Khmer, Thai, Lao). Brahmic Scripts were also used in the Philippines, Indonesia, Malaysia, etc., but many of these scripts have been replaced by the Latin alphabet following European colonization of the region.
Tibetan
Used as the national script in Bhutan, the Tibetan script is famous for its inefficiency when writing modern languages. Like with the modern English alphabet, many words written in this script require the inclusion of characters that are not pronounced in the words they spell out. This lack of phonetic accuracy is due to a lack of reform, so as the languages it represented changed, it did not change to match.
Devanagari
One of South Asia’s most popular scripts today, Devanagari is used to write Hindi, Nepali, Marthi and other less common languages. It’s also frequently used for printed Sanskrit. As a result, it is one of the most prominent scripts used in the modern age.

Latin


100 BCE - Current
Latin developed from a variety of written Greek used on the large island of Euboea. Sometime in the late first melenia BCE, colonists from the island settled in southern Italy, where it inspired the Etruscan script which in turn heavily influenced the creation of the Latin alphabet. (Fischer 137) Those scripts have long ago disappeared along with the languages they represented, as the Latin alphabet slowly replaced everything in its vicinity, like a volcanic explosion
Rustic Capitals
This is the oldest book-style lettering used for the Latin script. Like with Greek, there were no lowercase letters or spaces between words. This style was often carved into stone or written with ink for more formal purposes and later inspired printed humanist fonts as that technology developed in Europe. The popularity of these square capitals was associated with the Roman empire so other script styles grew in popularity as its influence waned.
Old Roman Cursive
One of the earliest examples of handwritten Latin was preserved as Graffiti under the lipili material covering the Roman city of Pompeii after the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 CE. This form of cursive is most illegible to modern reading, since a standard cursive style had not developed yet. Graffiti in Ancient Rome was not viewed as taboo. Almost every literate person would write on walls to make public statements.
Use in other languages
Originally designed for the Latin language, this script has spread further than any other in history. First with the spread of the Roman Empire, then the Christian faith, and European colonialism and its influence. Before printing, the style of the script differed regionally. Diacritics, letter combinations, and some unique letters were developed to represent sounds that were not present in Latin.

The Advent of Printing

Printing technologies have all developed in the last two millennia, representing what was probably the greatest technological advancement in written language since its inception. Printing remains an important process today, as written language has grown more prominent then ever. Printers' efficiency made information and access to the written word much more accessible. Probably facilitating the trend towards universal education and the high level of access we have to information today.
The form in which printing first appears for different scripts varies, in part due to the shape and construction each script. The first printed script (Chinese Hanzi) is written with individual characters for each word that each take up that same size and shape. Because of this, it made sense that entire words would be individually carved into equally sized stamps. By contrast, the Latin alphabet is written with symbols that represent individual sounds in a word. Letters were also not equal in size, complicating the typesetting process. Because of this, each word had to created using a group of stamps.

Block Printing
600-Present CE, Tang Dynasty
The first ever ink-printed materials are dated to the 7th century Tang Dynasty, China. The printing process was likely inspired by earlier seal rollers that existed as far back as the Sumerian Empire. The first prints created through this process taught about the history and principles of Buddhism.
Woodblock Printing
This is the oldest book-style lettering used for the Latin script. Like with Greek, there were no lowercase letters or spaces between words. This style was often carved into stone or written with ink for more formal purposes and later inspired printed humanist fonts as that technology developed in Europe. The popularity of these square capitals was associated with the Roman empire so other script styles grew in popularity as its influence waned.
Moveable type
Because of block printing limitations, a demand for a quicker printing system grew. As a result, moveable type for Chinese documents was invented around 1040 CE by a printer named Bi Sheng. This process of printing involved placing clay cubes, each featuring a character into a metal tray. The bottom of the tray was covered in a layer of resin, wax, and paper pulp that was heated up for the clay characters to stick to. After a print was made, this material could be reheated so clay characters could be removed and reused.

Letterpress


1400-Present CE, Mainz, Germany
The first letterpress in Europe is credited to Johannes Gutenberg in Mainz, Germany. While Guttenberg’s letterpress was not really the first example of moveable type, it marks an important change in typographic technology in Europe. It can give us a useful example of how writing spread at a wider scale can affect public sentiment for better and for worse. Notably, Gutenberg’s first publication using the printing press was a bible. So, like with earlier block printing, one of the main uses for letter printing was to spread religious beliefs. Another early printed publication, the Mellius Malifictarum, was cited by many religious and government officials in the 15th and 16th centuries, as evidence that Witchhunts were required to maintain civilization.
Typesetting
Early European letterpresses required type to be spaced manually, which was a tedious and time-consuming process. Each line of text had to be selected and spaced out manually using a composing stick before it could be placed in a pan as part of a page of text. However, even though the process was tedious, it was still quicker and appeared cleaner and more consistent than early block printing processes.
The first automatic typesetting printer, the linotype printer, appeared in the late 19th century, around the same time as personal typewriters. Though individual line spacing was automated, page composition, justification, etc., still had to be done manually.
Fonts
Fonts were organized into boxes, further split into individual letterforms that could be picked out to be placed in a line of text. These had to be cast in metal, and letters had to be cast many times so that many instances of the same letters could be stamped together.
Because of this, early printers were limited in the number of typefaces, font sizes, and styles that could be used because each font required considerable space and resources.

Modern Age

Historically, Writing had narrow use cases. With time, more and more complicated information was transmitted via the written word, which often required more efficient dictation processes to make their documentation economically feasible. Early printed books were often intended to help people recite works more accurately. As expected from this, many early print books were religious (popular as handwritten manuscripts beforehand as well).
In the modern era, printing has become a lot more commercial. Printing in bulk is more often used for advertising, journalism, etc. Also, as writing became more accessible, especially in this century, informal written communication has become a lot more prevalent. While it is by no means the first instance of this, social media and the web have increased the frequency of this form of writing.

Digitization of the Printing Process
Printing in the 20th century underwent a number of dramatic changes in the way type was created in bulk. At the beginning, the dominant method for print was hot-metal linotype printing. This shifted to phototypesetting in the mid-1900s and then quickly to digital typesetting by the mid to late 80s, which is still the main form of technology used for printing today.
Hot Metal Press
The linotype machine first appeared in the late 19th century and was the most popular form of typesetting until the mid 20th century. There were a lot of flaws with this process. Manual spacing between lines was still needed, printers had limited control over kerning, and it was still expensive and hard to procure some fonts. Also, because the linotype process involved connecting letters together using molten lead, it exposed a lot of printers to lead fumes.
The transition from linotype to phototypesetting also marked the end of unionized labor in print, since trained printers lost their leverage over publishers.
Phototypesetting
Phototypesetting was the leading form of typesetting for printed material for around 20 years before it was replaced by computer technology. The technology worked by projecting a disk/film of type onto lightsensitive paper to create typographic impressions This was more efficient than the earlier method, but justification, line spacing, etc., still had to be done manually. It had some other benefits over linotype. For instance, it made typefaces much easier to produce since an entire font could fit on a single strip of film. And because of the projection method of image transfer, fonts can be scaled within require a larger copy. Photographic typesetting was experimented with starting in the mid 1920s. It was used a lot for maps in WWII, and early commercial versions appeared in Japan after the War, but did not pick up in the US until the following decades.
Computer Printing
In the 70s, phototypesetters with a cathode ray screen starting appearing on the market. Computers were used in tandem with phototypesetters, where computers used to input edit and format type, phototypesetters used it translate information onto photosensitive paper. Computer used for typesetting also became commercially available around this time. Computers could justify and hyphenate type automatically, mostly replacing earlier typesetting options.

Digitization of Letterforms


Digital fonts have exploded since the advent of digital typography. In the early 1990s, there were fewer than 20,000 published fonts. That number has grown a hundred times over since the barrier to entry has lowered so much. There are a few main types of digital typefaces seen today.
Bitmap (Raster) Type
Made from pixels (or dot matrices as on Cathode Ray Screens), which are individually mapped out. These typefaces were easier to program and are typically easier to read on lower definition screens. However, they do not scale as vector fonts do and are more distinctly digital, making them less popular compared to vector typefaces.
Vector Type
Based on geometric shapes rather than individual pixels. These were less common in early computers because of their limitations on low-resolution, raster screens. Because most commercial computer screens are raster (pixel) based, vector images have to be rasterized for display. This process is called aliasing, which can look jagged, uneven, and confusing at lower resolutions.

Unicode


On the internet, characters needed to be encoded to be read properly and for fonts to be prescribed to them. There were a number of programs that existed to solve this problem in the early internet age, but they were not compatible with each other, and did not include a lot of scripts. Then in the early 90s Unicode became the main font encoding program for recognizing characters for the web. Characters are assigned a number that is read by the computer. Modern computers all accept these numbers as character identification. Characters are different than fonts, which can be added separately using Support for a script is proposed and then possibly accepted by the Unicode Consortium. Currently, Unicode supports over 170 scripts.
Geez (Ethiopic)
This script has been used in East Africa since the 1st century CE, though it has changed significantly in two thousand years. Its now the official script of Ethiopia and Eritrea. Notably, the script changed from an Abjad (i.e., Arabic, Hebrew, where only consonants are represented by letters) to an abugida (like the majority of scripts used in India referenced earlier). The script may have developed for the South Semitic script rather than the Phoenician script which makes it unique among modern phonetic scripts.
It was added to Unicode in 1999.

Cuneiform
Multiple Cuneiform scripts have been added to Unicode based on the work of a few Cuneiform scholars. The script is among a number of historical scripts (i.e., Phonetian, Ogham, Germanic and Turkic Runes, Linear B, etc) which are not in common use. These scripts are likely supported to make historical digitization and education easier in a web format. Versions of Cuneiform were added to Unicode in 2005 and 2006.
Cherokee
The history of the Cherokee syllabary is quite interesting. It was first invented in the 17th century when its inventor, a previously illiterate man named Sequoyah, was inspired by the Latin script to create a writing system for his own language. The visual influence from Latin is quite obvious, but those similarities are misleading since shared symbols do not represent the same sounds. Unlike the Latin Script, Cherokee is written with a syllabary where each character represents an entire syllable. Cherokee is used on infrastructure in some parts of Oklahoma and North Carolina. There have been some efforts to digitize historical documents written in Cherokee, so having an accessible digital version is useful.
The script was added to Unicode in 1999.
Emojis
Emojis were first designed by Shigetaka Kurita in 1999 for the Japanese mobile internet carrier “i-mode”. Emojis quickly became popular in Japan for their ability to add nuance to written messages. By the mid-2000s international companies like Apple and Google had taken an interest in emojis and a team of Apple employees submitted a list of 625 emojis to be incorporated into the Unicode Standard. This meant a standard set of emojis were encoded across all devices and platforms that used Unicode and helped legitimize the use of emojis. Many more emojis have been accepted by Unicode since then.
Deseret
The Deseret script was originally created by Mormon leadership in the mid 19th century as an attempt to create a more phonetically accurate script for the English language. This was part of a large attempt by There have been many attempts to create more accurate English writing systems, especially to signify the separation of societies. For instance, Ben Franklin also attempted to make a phonetic English alphabet for the newly created United States. Despite great efforts from the Church, the script did not catch on for a number of reasons. Mainly, printing and educating using the script would have been extremely costly. Because of the increased printing costs, books created with the script were often too pricey to be purchased by the Mormon membership.
Recent resurgences in interest in the Deseret script have led to digitization efforts. This has included the creation of new typefaces from the script and its addition to Unicode as of 2001.
Mongolian Traditional Script
While Mongolia has mostly used Cyrillic as its official script, there have been recent efforts to reintroduce the old script have led to its recognition as an official script. Mongolia’s traditional script is unique as one of (if not the only) vertically-connected alphabets in use. Because of these characteristics, the script has been notoriously difficult to adapt to a digital medium. Even though the Mongolian script was officially added to Unicode in 1999, it has not been successful in supporting the script's use in most web contexts.
Chinese Script(Hanzi, Hanja, Kanji)
Because of this script's large character inventory. It has been difficult to develop a good system for inputting the script into a computer. There have been attempts to create a keyboard for inputting Chinese characters, but the results have been too large and difficult to operate. Different systems have been tried, but the more popular today involves inputting Latinized versions of characters (i.e., Pinyin for Mandarin) that are converted digitally into the original script. In Taiwan, it is more common to phonetically input Mandarin using the Bopomofo (Zhuyin) script.
Both scripts were added to Unicode in 1993.

The Future



Unify


Because of the Latin script's ubiquity, its long history of typographic standards and printing, and more recently, its wide support on the internet, the script's influence continues to spread and represent more and more languages. Some argue that using a unified script for the majority of languages can help people across the world communicate better, learn other languages faster, etc. From a more practical perspective, scripts, like Mongolian, have had difficulties transferring writing to a digital format. Developing infrastructure for a new script is surprisingly expensive, possibly in the millions to create fonts, encoding, and printed documents, which can be enough to dissuade smaller communities or governments from using a unique script.

Diverify

By contrast, supporting a script and being able to print with it in bulk has never been easier. Historically, fonts for print needed to be carved in wood or cast in metal to be printed. That process requires materials and time. Making text documents digitally is faster and cheaper than ever before. Reviving or creating a script is attractive as a signal of community, especially as writing becomes more globally prominent. A unique script can help create a sense of identity so it makes sense that new or revived scripts have been gaining popularity.

Return to Analog

While this is unlikely, it's possible that the current digital age, and perhaps even the high levels of literacy seen today, could disappear if the large amounts of resources needed to maintain it went away. Digital text does not preserve well. Paper fares a bit better, but might not last more than a few hundred years. What written artifacts from today's writings will remain in the next hundred years? What about the next thousand years? How might preservation (or lack thereof) affect the way future people perceive the present?
